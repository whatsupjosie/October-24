# Fix the Light Path, Then Stage the Metal

The biggest visible gain available to the Foresight Counter comes from fixing the light path, not from adding a new effect. Composite every tray in linear light into a floating-point offscreen buffer. Tone map once at the end with Khronos PBR Neutral instead of the current per-fragment ACES fit. Dither the final 8-bit write. Replace 2x supersampling with exact per-pixel edge coverage from the distance field at native resolution. These changes cost zero downloaded bytes and cut GPU pixel work about fourfold. The second gain is in the metal. Polished metal and gloss look like whatever they reflect, so the rims need a procedurally generated photo studio (dark room, long strip softboxes, black bands), prefiltered on the GPU at startup. They also need a modern metal shader: energy-compensated GGX, measured gold and silver reflectance with edge tint, a brushing direction taken from the tray outline, and specular anti-aliasing so highlights hold still while trays move. The third gain is weight, and weight comes from height. Each tray is a vertical-walled extrusion of a 2D shape, so its 2D distance field is also its exact 3D distance below the rim. That makes physically correct soft shadows that sharpen at the contact line, plus closed-form ambient occlusion, cost only a handful of texture reads per pixel. The fourth gain is in the surfaces. They stop reading as computer-generated once value noise is replaced by gradient or simplex noise, detail is band-limited to the pixel, and materials are baked once into textures at load. Every recommended technique except one optional 64 KB area-light table is generated on the user's own GPU, so the bandwidth limit costs no quality. One caution applies to everything above: only some pipeline facts (browser support, documentation) were checked against their sources, and most shading formulas were written from memory of standard references that could not be reopened. Nothing was timed on an integrated GPU, so the first engineering task is a GPU timer and every derived formula needs a visual check.

## The current pipeline throws away quality before any effect runs

Today the renderer applies an ACES curve and a 2.2 gamma inside each tray's fragment shader, then blends the premultiplied results into an 8-bit buffer. Blending computes `dst = src + (1 − a)·dst`. When src and dst are already tone-mapped and gamma-encoded, the result is a blend of two curved signals, not the curve of the blended light [derived]. The visible symptoms follow from that [derived]:

- Anti-aliased edges and translucent glass get dark, muddy fringes, because coverage is mixed in the wrong space.
- Overlapping glows clip at 1.0 per object instead of adding, so highlights look like flat stickers rather than light.
- No value above 1.0 survives, so no physical bloom can be built later.
- ACES contrast is applied once per layer, so it compounds wherever layers overlap.

The fix is to composite everything in linear light into a floating-point offscreen target and tone map once, in a final full-screen pass. That is now safe to assume. **EXT_color_buffer_float is present on 99.95% of WebGL2 browsers** in Web3DSurvey's data, including 99.98% on Windows, though the page gives no sample date ([Web3DSurvey](https://web3dsurvey.com/webgl2/extensions/EXT_color_buffer_float)) [verified]. With it, RGBA16F and R11F_G11F_B10F become renderable and their outputs are not clamped ([Khronos registry](https://registry.khronos.org/webgl/extensions/EXT_color_buffer_float/)) [verified]. The WebGL working group's own discussion states that "resolving in non-linear spaces … gives incorrect results. Linear color spaces are recommended" ([KhronosGroup/WebGL PR #3668](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/WebGL/pull/3668)) [verified]. The pmndrs post-processing stack, the de facto three.js standard, calls half-float buffers "the preferred option for HDR-like workflows on desktop devices" and puts tone mapping only at the end of the pipeline ([pmndrs/postprocessing](https://github.com/pmndrs/postprocessing)) [verified]. The bench is opaque, so the scene buffer needs no alpha channel. That means **R11F_G11F_B10F carries HDR at 4 bytes per pixel, the same bandwidth as RGBA8** and half that of RGBA16F [derived]. Nobody re-checked whether blending into it works without the separate EXT_float_blend extension, so test framebuffer status and a blend readback at startup [recalled].

The tone mapper is the curve that squeezes unbounded light into the screen's range. It matters more for this brief than for most, because the product is accurate saturated color: jewel tones, gold, white marble. **Khronos PBR Neutral reproduces base color 1:1 up to a knee and compresses only the highlights.** It was built for faithful product color, runs as a 13-line GLSL sample, and has been adopted by three.js, Blender 4.2, Filament and Babylon.js ([GameDev.net](https://gamedev.net/news/khronos-pbr-neutral-tone-mapper-released-for-true-to-life-color-rendering-of-3d-objects-r2733); [Khronos](https://www.khronos.org/news/permalink/khronos-pbr-neutral-tone-mapper-released-for-true-to-life-color-rendering-of-3d-products)) [verified]. The same announcement says ACES has a limited range of reachable colors, especially bright yellow, green and cyan [verified]. Bevy's engine documentation is blunter. It says the common ACES fit "is not neutral": it shifts bright greens and reds toward orange and bright blues toward magenta, and it adds contrast. It describes AgX as "very neutral" with little hue shift, but deliberately desaturated and in need of a LUT ([Bevy docs](https://doc.qu1x.dev/bevy_trackball/bevy/core_pipeline/tonemapping/enum.Tonemapping.html)) [verified]. Don McCurdy of three.js describes AgX as a low-contrast base meant to be graded, and tone mapping as "practically free" in cost ([three.js forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/tone-mapping-overview/75204)) [verified]. For Foresight's materials, ACES turns gold highlights lemon-flat and pushes emerald and sapphire off-hue. PBR Neutral keeps those hues and keeps white marble neutral [derived]. Ship PBR Neutral as the default. An optional "cinematic" mode can use AgX plus a small grade, but AgX ports vary in correctness ([three.js forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/is-agx-tonemapping-implemented-correctly/60609)) [verified]. The exact PBR Neutral constants were not confirmed this session, so copy them from the Khronos ToneMapping repository, not from these notes.

The pixelated look has a second cause. With 2x supersampling, a hard or fixed-width edge gets at most four coverage levels per output pixel. The browser's bilinear downscale of an oversized canvas is not a proper resolve, and 8-bit quantization of already-curved gradients stair-steps them [derived]. Analytic coverage takes the signed distance, divides it by its screen-space rate of change, and ramps it across about one pixel. That gives roughly 256 coverage levels per edge at native resolution. Red Blob Games tested the ramp width: **0.5 px looks blocky, 2.0 px looks blurry, and 1.2 px is right** (1.25–1.3 px possibly better for large shapes), with results varying by browser and OS ([Red Blob Games](https://redblobgames.com/blog/2024-09-22-sdf-antialiasing)) [verified]. Dropping supersampling also removes the single largest cost. A 1440p display rendered at 2x is 14.7 megapixels, so one full-screen RGBA8 write is 59 MB before any blending overdraw, against 14.7 MB at native size [derived]. Other anti-aliasing options add little here:

- MSAA does nothing for edges that a distance field draws inside a quad [recalled].
- FXAA or SMAA belong only as a last resort for leftover texture shimmer, and SMAA needs lookup textures of tens of KB [recalled].
- Temporal anti-aliasing ghosts on moving UI, so skip it [derived].

Keep 2x only as a stills or screenshot mode, and downsample explicitly in a shader [derived].

Two more zero-cost fixes close out the pipeline. First, replace the 2.2 power curve with the exact piecewise sRGB curve. The pure power curve crushes the darkest values, which is where banding shows (standard practice; no source fetched) [recalled]. Second, add a ±1-step triangular-noise dither right before the 8-bit write. Bart Wronski showed that white noise leaves clumps and bands, while triangular-distribution noise fixes them "without over-noising the image," and blue noise is better still if a small texture is affordable ([Wronski](https://bartwronski.com/2016/10/30/dithering-part-three-real-world-2d-quantization-dithering/)) [verified]. A hashed pixel coordinate gives triangular noise with zero download. A 64×64 blue-noise tile would be roughly 4–16 KB [estimate]. Fade the dither out toward pure black and pure white, as a commenter on Wronski's post suggests, so the bench's deepest shadows stay black [verified].

Wider color and HDR output are not worth pursuing yet:

- **Display P3.** Wide-gamut output is broadly available in WebGL (Baseline 2024) ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/WebGLRenderingContext/drawingBufferColorSpace)) [verified]. It only pays off if materials are authored beyond sRGB, and it needs a matrix conversion before encoding [derived].
- **HDR displays.** True above-white output exists only for WebGPU canvases in Chromium, since Chrome 129 ([Chrome](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/new-in-webgpu-129)) [verified]. WebGL's equivalent is still an unmerged proposal ([PR #3668](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/WebGL/pull/3668)) [verified].
- **WebGPU.** It now ships in Chrome and Edge on Windows, in Firefox on Windows since version 141, and in Safari 26, according to a status page last edited May 28, 2026 ([gpuweb wiki](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/wiki/implementation-status)) [verified, possibly stale]. For a workload of blended quads and a few full-screen passes, it offers **no image-quality gain over a correct WebGL2 pipeline**, and it would mean maintaining two backends [derived].

Stay on WebGL2.

## Metal looks like whatever it reflects, so build the studio it reflects

Polished metal has no appearance of its own beyond its base reflectance; it shows what surrounds it. three.js's physical-material documentation says it in one line: "For best results, always specify an environment map" ([three.js](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/MeshPhysicalMaterial.html)) [verified]. The drei Lightformer approach "allows you to create an HDR in realtime using specific shapes that emit light", so a few emissive shapes stand in for a photograph ([Babylon.js forum](https://forum.babylonjs.com/t/babylonjs-equivalent-to-three-js-lightformer/55176)) [verified]. Product photographers do the same thing with real equipment. They use long strip softboxes; one standard model is 100×35 cm, about 3:1 ([Park Cameras](https://www.parkcameras.com/shop/Elinchrom-Rotalux-Strip-100x35cm-Softbox_E355141E)) [verified from listing title]. They also use graduated "gradient reflection" panels ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2014/04/02/tutorial-create-perfect-gradient-reflection-product-photography)) [recalled: title confirmed, body not read]. KeyShot, the standard product renderer, treats studio lighting presets as the core of product and jewelry work ([KeyShot](https://www.keyshot.com/blog/using-lighting-presets-in-keyshot)) [recalled: title confirmed only]. An environment that is uniformly bright or smoothly varying makes chrome read as grey plastic. Metal needs high dynamic range and hard-edged dark bands in what it reflects [derived]. That is why the researchers judged that **a curated environment raises perceived quality more than any texture upgrade** [derived].

The existing procedural studio function should be reshaped into a photographer's set [derived]:

- a near-black room at 0.01–0.03 relative luminance
- one key strip at 20–50× the background, with a 1:3 to 1:6 aspect and a soft edge about 5–10% of its width
- a kicker strip on the opposite side at 0.3–0.6×, slightly cooler, for warm/cool separation on silver
- a large overhead gradient scrim, for smooth sweeps across flat rim crests
- explicit zero-luminance black bands between the lights, which draw dark contour lines on bevels
- a dim floor bounce in the tray-lining color

The key strip follows the user's key-light direction and color temperature. Placement follows a hard geometric rule for a straight-down camera: **a rim facet tilted at angle θ reflects the direction 2θ from vertical**, in the compass direction the outline faces [derived]. A profile whose steepest slope is 30° only ever sees out to 60° from vertical. Strips must therefore sit at about 35–55° to land on its shoulders, and lights outside that band never appear on the rim at all [derived]. The outline's facing direction rotates around each polygon, so every side of every tray catches a different part of the studio. The researchers single this out as the main "it's real" cue [derived].

The standard way to light with that environment is the split-sum approximation from Unreal Engine 4. It pre-blurs the environment into a mip chain indexed by roughness, and stores the rest of the reflection integral as a small 2D table indexed by view angle and roughness ([Karis 2013](https://cgvr.informatik.uni-bremen.de/teaching/cg_literatur/Brian%20Karis%20-%20Real%20Shading%20in%20Unreal%20Engine%204,%202013%20Siggraph%20Course.pdf)) [recalled]. Karis later published an analytic fit of about 10 instructions that replaces the table entirely ([Karis 2014](https://www.unrealengine.com/en-US/blog/physically-based-shading-on-mobile)) [recalled]. Diffuse light reduces to nine spherical-harmonic coefficients, evaluated in about 20 multiply-adds ([Ramamoorthi & Hanrahan](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/envmap/)) [recalled, link unconfirmed]. All three pieces can be generated on the GPU at startup. A 128² RGBA16F cubemap with its mip chain takes about **1.05 MB of video memory and zero downloaded bytes** [derived]. Re-prefiltering it with 32–64 importance samples per texel was estimated at roughly 1 ms on an Iris Xe-class GPU, which would let the environment follow the light live as the user drags it [estimate, unbenchmarked]. A 32×32 table is likely indistinguishable from 128², because the function is smooth [derived]. A downloaded HDRI is optional. Poly Haven's studio HDRIs are CC0 ([Poly Haven FAQ](https://polyhaven.com/faq)) [recalled], but a 1K file was expected to be 1–2 MB [estimate, unverified], and the procedural set is easier to control.

The metal shader needs four upgrades. All of them are cheap and none needs a download.

1. **Energy compensation.** GGX is the standard microfacet model. In its single-bounce form it loses the light that bounces between microfacets, so rough and satin gold, brass and bronze come out too dark and too grey. Kulla and Conty's Imageworks course notes show the loss, and show that the corrected multi-bounce term also makes colored metals more saturated ([Kulla & Conty 2017](https://blog.selfshadow.com/publications/s2017-shading-course/imageworks/s2017_pbs_imageworks_slides.pdf)) [recalled]. Filament's fix is one line, `energyCompensation = 1.0 + f0 * (1.0 / dfg.y - 1.0)`, applied to both the key light and the environment ([Filament](https://google.github.io/filament/Filament.md.html)) [recalled]. It costs about 3 instructions [estimate]. Fdez-Agüera's fuller version also conserves energy in the diffuse of marble and wood floors ([JCGT 2019](https://www.jcgt.org/published/0008/01/03/paper.pdf)) [recalled]. The visible change is largest on brushed and satin finishes and nearly zero on mirror polish [derived].
2. **Measured reflectance.** The commonly tabulated linear values are gold (1.000, 0.782, 0.344), silver (0.972, 0.960, 0.915), copper (0.955, 0.638, 0.538) and brass (0.910, 0.778, 0.423) ([Filament](https://google.github.io/filament/Filament.md.html)) [recalled; spot-check every row]. These notes found no measured data for rose gold, bronze or champagne, so those finishes are art-directed blends tuned by eye [derived].
3. **Edge tint.** Hoffman's F82 model subtracts a small correction term so reflectance dips realistically near grazing angles. That dip gives gold and copper their colored edges ([Hoffman](https://renderwonk.com/publications/wp-generalization-adobe/gen-adobe.pdf)) [recalled]. OpenPBR and Blender's Principled shader use this form ([OpenPBR paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.23696); [Blender PR #112551](https://projects.blender.org/blender/blender/pulls/112551)) [recalled]. Hoffman also argues that plugging RGB complex refractive indices into the full physical equations is less accurate, not more ([Hoffman 2019](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.2312/mam20191305)) [recalled]. F82 costs about 15 instructions against 6 for plain Schlick [estimate], plus one extra channel in the startup-generated table to keep the environment term consistent [derived].
4. **Brushing direction from the outline.** The gradient of the tray's 2D distance field gives a tangent. That tangent can run around the rim (circumferential brushing) or across it (sunburst). It stays continuous through rounded corners, so it is seam-free [derived]. Filament's anisotropic GGX plus its "bent reflection vector" trick stretches both direct and environment highlights along that tangent, for about 25 extra instructions ([Filament](https://google.github.io/filament/Filament.md.html)) [recalled; cost estimate].

The rim's shape is what those highlights trace. Treat the rim cross-section as a 2D profile swept along the polygon's distance field, `tray(p) = prof(vec2(d2(p.xy), p.z))`. This generalizes Quilez's extrusion operator ([IQ, distance functions](https://iquilezles.org/articles/distfunctions/)) [recalled, link unconfirmed; the sweep itself is derived]. Seen straight down, the swept rim is just a height field, so it needs no ray marching. The normal comes analytically from the profile's slope times the outline gradient. That costs one polygon-distance evaluation, against the four to six that numerical normals need [derived]; Quilez's cheapest numerical method still takes four ([IQ, normals](https://iquilezles.org/articles/normalsSDF/)) [recalled]. The profile shape controls the highlights [derived]:

- a small, high-curvature bead throws a thin bright line
- a broad cove throws a soft gradient
- bead, cove, bead in sequence gives **the multi-line highlight of a moulded picture frame**

Concave corners mitre automatically, like a joined frame. Rounding them means inserting extra arc vertices on the CPU, because offsetting a distance field cannot round inward corners [derived]. The old skeuomorphic tricks (a bright top edge, a dark bottom line, an inner shadow in wells) all fall out of correct normals under one light. Do not paint them on as separate layers, or they will contradict the lighting when the light moves [derived].

Thin bright beads are exactly what sparkles and crawls when a tray moves, and a UI has no temporal anti-aliasing to hide it. Geometric specular anti-aliasing widens roughness according to how fast the normal changes across the pixel. Tokuyoshi and Kaplanyan's version is a few lines ([Tokuyoshi & Kaplanyan 2019](https://www.jp.square-enix.com/tech/library/pdf/ImprovedGeometricSpecularAA.pdf)) [recalled], building on Kaplanyan et al. ([NVIDIA 2016](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2016-06_Filtering-Distributions-of/NDFFiltering.pdf)) [recalled]. The recalled constants disagree (0.159 and 0.18 in the paper, 0.15 and 0.2 as Filament defaults) and need checking. Foresight's normals come from an analytic profile, so the variance can be computed exactly from the profile's curvature and the corner radius, instead of from blocky 2×2-pixel derivatives. That costs about 15 instructions and gives **a stable one-pixel highlight line instead of a dotted crawl** [derived; validate with a slowly rotating tray]. Take screen-space derivatives before any branch, because they are undefined inside divergent control flow [recalled].

For razor-sharp strip highlights with the correct shape, which also move without re-prefiltering, use Linearly Transformed Cosines (LTC). LTC shades a rectangular area light exactly and has run in WebGL2 for years through three.js's RectAreaLight ([Hill & Heitz 2016](https://www.advances.realtimerendering.com/s2016/s2016_ltc_rnd.pdf)) [recalled]. It needs **two 64×64 lookup tables, about 64 KB as half-floats**. That is the only asset in this report that cannot be generated at runtime, because the tables come from an offline fit [recalled; size derived]. It costs about 100 instructions and two texture reads per strip per pixel [estimate]. The license of the original tables was not confirmed, though three.js redistributes them under MIT. Karis's older "representative point" method gives approximate strip highlights with no table at all ([Karis 2013](https://cgvr.informatik.uni-bremen.de/teaching/cg_literatur/Brian%20Karis%20-%20Real%20Shading%20in%20Unreal%20Engine%204,%202013%20Siggraph%20Course.pdf)) [recalled]. With everything enabled, a rim pixel was estimated at about 250 instructions and 4–5 texture reads, well inside the budget set out in the build-order section [estimate].

## Weight comes from height, and vertical-walled trays make the physics cheap

Each tray is a polygon extruded straight up with vertical walls. For any point below rim height, the nearest point on the tray is on the wall at the same height. So **the tray's exact 3D distance equals its 2D polygon distance** [derived]. That turns Quilez's sphere-traced soft shadow into a short 2D march of about 8–16 steps [estimate]. The shadow marches toward the light and keeps the minimum of `k·h/t`, where h is the distance to the nearest surface and t is the distance traveled ([IQ, soft shadows](https://iquilezles.org/articles/rmshadows)) [recalled]. It produces contact hardening for free. Near the wall base t is tiny, so the shadow is sharp; at the tip t is large, so the shadow is soft [derived]. NVIDIA's PCSS needs a separate blocker search to fake the same behavior, using penumbra width `(d_receiver − d_blocker) · w_light / d_blocker` ([NVIDIA PCSS](https://developer.download.nvidia.com/shaderlibrary/docs/shadow_PCSS.pdf)) [recalled]. Quilez also gives two variants ([IQ, soft shadows](https://iquilezles.org/articles/rmshadows)) [recalled]:

- an improved version, credited to Sebastian Aaltonen's Claybook talk, which removes banding when the ray skips past an edge
- a further version that rolls the shadow smoothly into full umbra; its constants were held at medium confidence

The current shadow extrudes the outline along an offset vector. That is the hard-shadow limit of this answer: the shape is right, but the penumbra that grows with height is missing [derived]. For a cheap fallback, a Gaussian-blurred straight edge has an exact closed form, `0.5·erfc(d/(σ√2))`. It is too dark at convex corners until the footprint is rounded by about 1.2σ [derived]. Production UI renderers, from Flutter's Impeller to Evan Wallace's analytic shadows, use similar closed forms instead of real blurs ([Flutter rrect_blur](https://flutter.googlesource.com/mirrors/engine/+/HEAD/impeller/entity/shaders/rrect_blur.frag); [Evan Wallace](https://madebyevan.com/shaders/fast-rounded-rectangle-shadows/)) [recalled].

Ambient occlusion measures how much of the sky a point can see. For a ground point at distance x from a long wall of height H it has an exact formula: **occlusion = 0.5·(1 − x/√(x² + H²))** [derived; validate once against a brute-force integration]. It gives 0.5 at the wall base (the dark contact crease), 0.146 one rim height out, and 0.026 three rim heights out. The skirt's width therefore scales with rim height, so taller trays automatically get wider, softer skirts. That is a direct weight cue for the price of one distance evaluation and one inverse square root [derived]. The same formula covers two more cases [derived]:

- When two trays nearly touch, their facing walls block separate parts of the sky, so their terms add exactly, and the gap visibly pinches light as the trays are dragged together.
- Inside a tray, the formula applied against the inner rim gives the recessed, heavy "dish" look.

The research benchmark for generic screen-space occlusion is Activision's GTAO, reported at about 0.5 ms at 1080p on a PS4 ([GTAO paper](https://www.iryoku.com/downloads/Practical-Realtime-Strategies-for-Accurate-Indirect-Occlusion.pdf)) [recalled]. Intel's reference port, XeGTAO, is written in compute shaders, which WebGL2 lacks ([XeGTAO](https://github.com/GameTechDev/XeGTAO)) [recalled]. Keep it as a fallback only if tray contents grow complex (gems, screws, inlays).

How the pieces combine matters as much as the pieces themselves. CSS-style UI shadows stack several darkening layers. Josh Comeau's widely cited rules are the right instincts: one consistent light direction; offset and blur that grow with elevation while opacity falls; and shadows tinted as a darker version of the surface rather than grey-black ([Comeau](https://www.joshwcomeau.com/css/designing-shadows)) [recalled]. Material Design formalizes them as a key light plus an ambient light ([Material](https://m1.material.io:443/material-design/environment.html)) [recalled]. The physical version sums light instead: `color = albedo · (key · keyVisibility · N·L + sky · skyVisibility + bounce)`. The soft shadow supplies key visibility and the wall formula supplies sky visibility. **Multiplying darkening layers double-darkens wherever they overlap; summing lights cannot** [derived]. Tinting falls out of the sum: shadowed walnut reads deep reddish-brown and shadowed indigo stone reads cool, never grey [derived]. Quilez raises the shadow term to a slightly different power per color channel, which warms the penumbra edge ([IQ, outdoor lighting](https://iquilezles.org/articles/outdoorslighting/)) [recalled, link unconfirmed].

The same wall formula also gives one bounce of colored light: replace the sky a neighbor blocks with that neighbor's own lit color. A red lacquer tray then tints the bench on its lit side only [derived]. Polished metal adds one more cue, which no source documents for UI. A vertical, mirror-like wall reflects key light down onto the bench on the lit side, out to a distance of `H · (L.xy · n) / L.z`. A brass rim therefore throws **a warm glow strip on one side and a shadow on the other**, and that pairing reads as heavy polished metal [derived].

Apple's Liquid Glass is the one shipping system built explicitly around depth, and in it size and light carry weight. According to WWDC25 ([Meet Liquid Glass](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/)) [verified]:

- larger elements get "more pronounced lensing and refraction effects, and a softer scattering of light," plus "deeper, richer shadows" to simulate thicker material
- light sources "move in space, causing light to travel around the material, defining its silhouette"
- on touch, the material "illuminates from within," and the glow spreads "onto any Liquid Glass elements nearby"

Apple published no numeric parameters [verified]. For Foresight, rim thickness, wall height, shadow depth and highlight sharpness should all scale with tray size, so large trays feel heavier and not just bigger [derived]. The pick-up and put-down cycle is the strongest weight cue. Drive one real height parameter h, and everything else follows [derived]:

- the shadow offset becomes `h · L.xy / L.z`
- the penumbra widens by about `h · tan(light radius)`
- the occlusion skirt widens and fades
- the contact line disappears
- the tray scales by `D/(D − h)`, about 4% for a lift of one rim height at a viewing distance of 25 rim heights

A fast lift, a slight overshoot and a slower settle make mass legible. Heavier trays should lift lower and overshoot less [derived].

Bench reflections should stay subtle, and the physics agrees. Glass, polished stone and lacquer (refractive index about 1.5) reflect about **4% at near-normal viewing** ([Schlick's approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlick%27s_approximation)) [recalled, link unconfirmed; arithmetic derived]. Seen straight down, an object's mirror image is hidden directly beneath it, and visible reflection comes only from the slight oblique view offset [derived]. On dark indigo stone, a 4% reflection of a bright brass rim is as bright as the stone itself and reads strongly. On white marble it nearly vanishes except at highlights. So scale reflection strength by how dark the bench is [derived]. Blur should grow with height above the bench, sharp at the rim base and soft at the top, as on a real polished table. With analytic trays that is a second short trace of 6–12 steps inside each tray's quad, or a half-resolution objects layer sampled at a mip level matched to the blur [derived; cost estimate].

The selected tray's backlight should become real light. Author it as HDR emission above 1.0 and let bloom produce the glow, replacing the analytic halos. Jimenez's bloom from Call of Duty: Advanced Warfare is the reference ([Jimenez, iryoku.com](https://www.iryoku.com/?p=1707)) [verified]:

- a downsample pyramid whose first step weights samples by `1/(1 + luma)` to suppress fireflies
- a 3×3 tent-filter upsample
- **no threshold**: "the blurred bloom layer is set to a low value (say 0.04)", so only very bright pixels visibly glow

The metal-shading thread suggested a threshold near 1.0, from memory. The verified source argues against one, and with no threshold, glow cannot pop on and off as trays are dragged [derived]. Starting at half resolution, the chain touches about one third of the full-resolution pixel count per direction, roughly 10 MB of writes per frame at 1440p [estimate]. Add the emission as light onto the bench and neighbors through the same form-factor formula, so the selection visibly lights its surroundings instead of wearing an outline [derived].

## Surfaces stop reading as computer-generated once noise is band-limited and baked

The current textures look computer-generated mainly because of value noise. Value noise clusters its features on the integer grid, which shows as faint axis-aligned blockiness. With cubic smoothing, it also creases the lighting at cell borders [derived]. Lagae and colleagues' survey of procedural noise states the underlying point: noise can be anti-aliased only to the degree it is band-limited. Gradient (Perlin) noise roughly is; value noise is worse ([Lagae et al. 2010](https://diglib.eg.org:443/handle/10.2312/CGF.v29i8pp2579-2600)) [recalled]. Gustavson and McEwan's psrdnoise is simplex noise published as GLSL ([JCGT 2022](https://jcgt.org/published/0011/01/02/paper-lowres.pdf)) [recalled]. It evaluates 3 corners in 2D and 4 in 3D (against 4 and 8 for lattice noise), and it offers tiling, an animated "flow" swirl and analytic gradients. The LYGIA library ports it, along with Quilez's band-limited cosine ([LYGIA fcos](https://git.gay/kaneraZh/lygia/src/tag/1.4.1/math/fcos.glsl)) [recalled]. Rotate each octave by about 37° and use a lacunarity of 2.01, so the octave grids never line up [derived convention].

Geological structure comes from two techniques:

- Quilez's two-level domain warping: five fbm evaluations, whose intermediate vectors double as color controls ([IQ, warp](https://iquilezles.org/articles/warp/)) [recalled]
- ridged multifractals, which turn octaves into sharp, branching creases for veins ([Book of Shaders](https://thebookofshaders.com/13/)) [recalled]

Every periodic term must fade to its average once its frequency is higher than a pixel can show. Quilez's `fcos(x) = cos(x)·sin(w/2)/(w/2)`, with `w = fwidth(x)`, does this for vein and ring sines ([IQ, bandlimiting](https://iquilezles.org:443/articles/bandlimiting/)) [recalled]. Fading each fbm octave by `1 − smoothstep(0.25, 0.5, frequency·w)` does it for noise [derived]. Unfiltered detail shimmers when trays move, and shimmer reads cheap.

That richness is too expensive to evaluate every frame. A two-level warp was estimated at 1,500–2,500 operations per pixel, and numerical normals multiply that 3–5×, which is at or past an integrated GPU's whole budget [estimate]. The bench and tray surfaces are static, so **render the procedural materials once at load into textures**: 2048² for the bench and 512–1K for rims and trays [derived]. Supersample during the bake for free anti-aliasing. Write height into an R16F target, compute normals by central differences in a second pass, and build a mip chain with `generateMipmap` [derived]. Download stays at zero, runtime cost drops to a texture fetch, and the bake can afford octave-heavy recipes that per-frame shading never could. The researchers called this the single most important architectural decision for the target [derived]. Do not derive normals from `dFdx`/`dFdy` of a procedural height. Those derivatives are constant across each 2×2-pixel block, which produces blocky, zoom-dependent faceting in highlights [derived]. Stack detail layers (macro bump, micro-normal, clear-coat ripple) with Mikkelsen's surface-gradient framework or with Reoriented Normal Mapping, not by adding normals ([Mikkelsen 2020](https://jcgt.org/published/0009/03/04/paper.pdf); [Barré-Brisebois & Hill](https://blog.selfshadow.com/publications/blending-in-detail)) [recalled].

The convincing recipes come from material physics. The researchers built each one from the cited models.

- **Polished white marble** [derived]. A warm-white base around 0.80–0.88, ridged-multifractal primary veins on a warped domain, Voronoi-edge hairlines, and a deeper vein layer blurred and desaturated to 40–60% contrast. Polish it to a roughness of 0.03–0.08, slightly rougher inside the veins. One contradiction between threads needs flagging. The materials thread offsets that deep layer by the view direction to create parallax, but with the camera looking straight down that offset is zero. The shadow thread caught this, and says blur and desaturation alone still read as veins inside a translucent volume [derived]. True parallax appears only with the oblique offset or a pointer-driven view vector.
- **Walnut.** Model the tree, not the board. A 3D ring field around a pith axis, tilted 1–3° to the board plane and set a few centimeters below it, produces cathedral arches automatically ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.04224)) [recalled]. Pores are Worley dots stretched 15–30:1 along the grain. The shimmering "figure" of finished wood is an anisotropic highlight from fibers beneath the finish, per Marschner et al. ([Marschner 2005](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~srm/publications/SG05-wood.html)) [recalled].
- **Indigo stone** [derived]. There are two physically grounded directions: porphyry-like speckles from two scales of Worley cells, or charoite-like swirling fibers with a highlight that follows the warp. Charoite is the more hyper-real choice.

Over all floors, add Filament's clear coat: a second, sharp reflection lobe at 4% that dims the base by its own Fresnel term. **It is the single biggest win for marble, varnished wood and lacquer** ([Filament](https://google.github.io/filament/Filament.md.html)) [recalled]. Restraint is part of every recipe. Luxury product images are more controlled than reality, not more accurate, and wear or smudges belong only in roughness (±0.03–0.08), below conscious notice [derived].

**Pearl.** Pearl's color shift is thin-film interference. Belcour and Barla's 2017 model replaces the Fresnel term with an analytic thin-film reflectance that is already anti-aliased across the spectrum, so it works in RGB at real-time cost ([Belcour & Barla](https://blog.unity.com/es/technology/a-practical-extension-to-microfacet-theory-for-the-modeling-of-varying-iridescence)) [recalled]. three.js exposes it with defaults of film index 1.3 and thickness 100–400 nm ([three.js](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/MeshPhysicalMaterial.html)) [verified]. For a fixed film, the result depends only on angle and thickness. A 64×64 table baked at load (32 KB of video memory) therefore reduces it to one fetch [derived]. Banding the thickness into 6–12 soft terraces of warped noise mimics stacked nacre platelets [derived]. A single film only approximates real nacre, and no source in the notes covers multilayer models.

**Jewel glass.** Jewel glass deepens in color with thickness through Beer–Lambert absorption (three.js's `attenuationColor` and `attenuationDistance`) ([three.js](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/MeshPhysicalMaterial.html)) [verified], so bevels read richer than centers. Foresight draws the bench itself, so refraction can re-evaluate the procedural bench at the bent coordinate, with no extra render pass [derived]. Where a buffer is needed, drei's MeshTransmissionMaterial shows that one shared low-resolution buffer works, and that with roughness "a very small buffer (for example, 32x32) still looks good" ([drei docs](https://drei.docs.pmnd.rs/shaders/mesh-transmission-material)) [verified]. liquidGL's parameter model is a ready starting point for rim glass ([liquidGL](https://github.com/naughtyduk/liquidGL)) [verified]:

- base refraction 0.01
- extra refraction of 0.08 within a bevel band 15% of the short side wide
- a color split that scales with the same offset

Restrict dispersion to bevel pixels [derived].

**Translucent stone and pearl.** Jade, marble and pearl should cast lighter, tinted shadows: multiply visibility by transmittance `exp(−σ·path)` instead of zeroing it. The researchers judged that this cue alone removes most of the plastic look [derived]. Wrapped diffuse and Frostbite's thickness-based translucency are the cheap shading terms ([GPU Gems ch. 16](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems/part-iii-materials/chapter-16-real-time-approximations-subsurface-scattering); [Frostbite](https://frostbite.com/frostbite/news/approximating-translucency-for-a-fast-cheap-and-convincing-subsurface-scattering-look)) [recalled].

**Sparkle.** Mica and metallic-flake sparkle can be a hashed per-cell glint of 30–60 instructions that fades out below about 1.5 pixels per cell [derived]. The research line runs from Zirr and Kaplanyan (2016) to Deliot and Belcour (2023). The heavier methods are worth adopting only if the cheap version flickers ([Deliot & Belcour](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05051)) [recalled].

Scanned textures lose on both counts that matter here. ambientCG and Poly Haven are CC0 ([license guide](https://licenseorg.com/guide/3d-assets/ambientcg); [Poly Haven](https://polyhaven.com/faq)) [recalled]. A 1K texture set compressed to KTX2 was estimated at 0.5–1 MB, plus a Basis Universal transcoder of perhaps a few hundred KB ([Basis Universal](https://skia.googlesource.com/external/github.com/BinomialLLC/basis_universal/+/refs/tags/v2_0)) [estimate, unmeasured]. Yet **a 1K marble scan stretched over a 1 m bench is about 1 mm per texel**, which is under-resolved for a close desktop view. Procedural veins have unlimited resolution [derived]. Long veins also break when de-tiled. Histogram-preserving blending (Heitz–Neyret, Burley) suits only uniform textures like granite grain ([Burley 2019](https://www.jcgt.org/published/0008/04/02/paper.pdf)) [recalled]. Make the default fully procedural, at zero bytes. Add at most one optional 512²–1K tileable micro-detail map (~200–400 KB), and only if stone grain or wood pores need it [estimate].

## Live screens stay HTML, so the frame and a gloss layer must sell the glass

Each tray's screen is live HTML, often an iframe, and that sets a hard limit. Chrome's HTML-in-Canvas API is the only way to put live, interactive DOM into a WebGL texture. It is still an origin trial that "runs through Chrome 160," and its API names were renamed in Chrome 150 and 155 ([Chrome blog, updates](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/html-in-canvas-ot-changes)) [verified]. Two limits rule it out as the base design. "Cross-origin iframe content isn't supported," and rendering runs on the main thread, so scrolling inside the content cannot update on its own ([Chrome blog, origin trial](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/html-in-canvas-origin-trial?hl=de)) [verified]. Snapshot libraries fit even worse:

- @ybouane/liquidglass opens one WebGL context per element, against a browser cap of about 16 contexts, and takes 100–500 ms to initialize ([README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@ybouane/liquidglass@1.0.3/README.md)) [verified]
- SVG-filter lensing through `backdrop-filter` works only in Chromium ([LogRocket](https://blog.logrocket.com/how-create-liquid-glass-effects-css-and-svg/)) [verified]

So **the WebGL renderer can never see the screen pixels**. Glass in the frame can refract only the bench and the other trays, which the renderer draws itself [derived].

The robust design stacks three layers [derived]:

1. **Below: the WebGL2 canvas.** It draws the bench, frames, rims, shadows, the selected tray's backlight, and a dark "cavity" gradient inside each opening.
2. **Middle: the DOM content.** Position it with a CSS transform and clip it with `clip-path: path()` to the rounded polygon opening. Re-clip it as trays morph, so the outline changes but the content is never warped. DOM can only follow affine or 3D transforms, not bends.
3. **Above: a gloss layer.** It is non-interactive (`pointer-events: none`) and carries three things: an inner shadow at the bezel edge so the screen sits recessed, a faint diagonal reflection blended with `screen`, and a thin bright line where glass meets metal.

One WebGL context cannot sit both below and above the DOM. Make the top layer CSS-only, so the page keeps a single context [derived]. This matters for the rule that the frame covers the screen's edges. The rim drawn in WebGL sits underneath the content, so **the clip path must land exactly on the rim's inner lip**, and the top layer's inner shadow supplies the visual overlap [derived]. Apple's rules fit this design ([WWDC25](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/)) [verified]:

- glass floats above content
- "Always avoid glass on glass"
- the two glass variants "should never be mixed"

That means one rim-glass recipe per tray, with opaque screens inside.

Pointer motion should move the viewer, not the scene. A small, eased shift of the view vector, used only for the specular and environment lookups, makes highlights glide across bevels as the pointer moves without competing with drags [derived]. liquidGL's defaults give a sensible scale: a 5° tilt and a 400 ms settle ([liquidGL](https://github.com/naughtyduk/liquidGL)) [verified]. The same vector should drive the CSS gloss offset, so WebGL and CSS highlights agree [derived].

Shipping systems all degrade gracefully, and Foresight should too.

- **Apple** makes glass frostier under Reduce Transparency, makes it black or white with borders under Increase Contrast, and turns off elastic motion under Reduce Motion ([WWDC25](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/)) [verified].
- **Windows** builds Acrylic from a fixed recipe of blur, exclusion blend, tint and noise. It swaps Acrylic for solid color under High Contrast, with transparency turned off, in Battery Saver, and on low-end hardware ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/windows/uwp/style/acrylic)) [verified].
- **The web** has the CSS `prefers-reduced-transparency` query. As of 2023 it worked in Chrome and Edge 118 but not in Safari ([Chrome blog](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/css-prefers-reduced-transparency)) [verified, dated].

Foresight therefore needs its own Full / Acrylic-style / Solid toggle, which doubles as the low-end GPU tier [derived]. Text never sits on refractive or animated glass. Test contrast against the worst-case backdrop pixel at the usual WCAG thresholds of 4.5:1 and 3:1 [recalled; WCAG text not re-fetched].

## A laptop GPU affords a few thousand operations per pixel: the build order

Everything above has to hold 60 fps on an integrated laptop GPU, and nobody measured anything. The researchers' arithmetic starts from 1440p at 60 fps, which is 221 million pixels per second. They assumed an Iris Xe-class GPU peaks near 2 TFLOPS (a figure none of them could source) and runs at 30–50% efficiency. That leaves **about 2,700–4,500 floating-point operations per pixel per frame, for everything** [estimate]. One exact 12-edge polygon distance costs roughly 150–200 operations, so the budget holds only 10–20 of them per pixel. A 20-step shadow march over 15 trays at full resolution would run 15–20× over budget [estimate].

The architecture that fits has four passes [derived]:

1. A quarter-resolution "scene field" each frame, storing distance to the nearest tray, its height and its id (estimated at a few percent of the GPU [estimate]).
2. A full-resolution bench pass whose shadows, occlusion and bounce read that field.
3. One quad per tray, for crisp silhouettes and rim shading.
4. The half-resolution bloom chain, feeding one final pass.

Render on demand, so an idle screen costs nothing, and cache the bench. While dragging, drop to 0.67–0.75× resolution and restore on release, sharpening with AMD's CAS, which expects linear input ([FidelityFX CAS](https://github.com/GPUOpen-LibrariesAndSDKs/FidelityFX-SDK/blob/main/docs/techniques/contrast-adaptive-sharpening.md)) [verified]. Create the context with `antialias: false`, `depth: false`, `stencil: false` and `powerPreference: "high-performance"` ([pmndrs](https://github.com/pmndrs/postprocessing)) [verified].

Measuring is practical on a Windows laptop. GPU timer queries (EXT_disjoint_timer_query_webgl2) work in **85.95% of tested Windows browsers, 94.19% of Edge and 81.1% of Chrome**, but almost never in Firefox or Safari. The app therefore also needs a frame-time fallback ([Web3DSurvey](https://web3dsurvey.com/webgl2/extensions/EXT_disjoint_timer_query_webgl2)) [verified].

The order below puts certainty and visible gain first. Steps 1–4 rest on verified sources, cost no bytes, and lower GPU load overall. Every later effect depends on them, because bloom, energy compensation and colored light all need values above 1.0 in a linear buffer. Steps 5–7 bring the largest visible jump in metal and weight. They rest on recalled formulas and the researchers' own derivations, so each needs a visual check as it lands.

On the evidence, leave these out [derived]:

- temporal anti-aliasing, depth of field and motion blur, which blur interactive targets and ghost on moving UI
- chromatic aberration on tray edges, which recreates the soft, fringed look Foresight is trying to escape
- full-screen clarity or sharpening at native resolution, which rings around metal edges
- SMAA and its lookup textures
- scanned textures, downloaded HDRIs and the Basis transcoder
- a WebGPU backend
- HTML-in-Canvas as anything more than a future experiment

If frame time runs over on weaker machines, degrade in this order [derived]:

1. Cut the shadow march from 16 steps to 8, or switch to the single-step analytic shadow.
2. Compute bench occlusion and shadow at half resolution, with distance-aware upsampling.
3. Show reflections only on dark benches.
4. Use fewer bloom mips.
5. Use dynamic resolution.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort should go. The hyper-real look Foresight is chasing comes mostly from correct light arithmetic and controlled reflections, not from more effects. The most certain steps (linear HDR, a neutral tone mapper, analytic edges, dithering, Jimenez bloom) are also the cheapest, and they make the renderer faster rather than slower. The geometry turns out to be an advantage. Trays are vertical-walled extrusions viewed from above, so the physically correct shadows, occlusion, bounce and reflections cost less than the layered fakes they replace. They also stay correct when trays lift, morph and collide, which stacked CSS-style layers cannot do. The bandwidth limit costs no quality either. Materials and environments generated on the GPU out-resolve anything a modest download could deliver, and the whole stack needs at most 64 KB of data.

The open risk is performance and correctness, not technique. Every GPU cost in this report is arithmetic. Three of the five threads worked from memory, and the central weight formulas are the researchers' own. The first day of work (timer queries, then the HDR pipeline) will show whether the budget is as roomy as estimated. Check each recalled constant against its source as it goes into the code, not afterward. That applies to PBR Neutral's curve, the metal reflectance table, the specular-AA constants and Quilez's umbra variant.
